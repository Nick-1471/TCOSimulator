Why fuel and electricity prices are adjustable

Fuel and electricity prices change often and lie outside any single forecast, so they are set as sliders you move to today's local values rather than baked-in constants:

  • Petrol is repriced every month. The Department of Mineral Resources and Energy sets the pump price on the first Wednesday of each month from the Basic Fuel Price (international petrol prices plus the rand/US dollar exchange rate) plus fixed taxes and levies. Coastal 95 ULP was about R25.23 in July 2026 and eased to about R24.33 in August 2026, so any fixed figure dates within weeks.
  • Electricity tariffs vary by municipality and reset annually. Nelson Mandela Bay moved to about R4.50/kWh on 1 July 2026; other metros and Eskom Direct customers differ. There is no single national retail rate.
  • Set your own numbers. Enter the current pump price and your municipal tariff and the model gives a result you can trust for today. The escalation sliders then project each price forward at your chosen annual rate, so the petrol-versus-electricity escalation gap still drives the long-run divergence.

Beyond CO₂, battery materials & disposal burden (kg of lithium-ion critical minerals)

Horizon ICE HEV EV grid EV solar
This is the footprint the CO₂ tables do not capture: lithium, nickel, cobalt, manganese, graphite and copper that must be mined, refined and eventually disposed of. Each EV build is ~185 kg of these minerals (a whole EV uses ~207 kg of minerals vs ~34 kg for a petrol car, about 6×, per the IEA); each home-storage battery adds ~55 kg and is replaced roughly every 12 years. The solar EV, the CO₂ winner, carries the heaviest materials burden of all (car battery + home storage + every replacement), which is precisely why a CO₂-only headline can be greenwashing. The ICE figure is ~0 here because its lead-acid starter battery sits in a mature ~99%-recycled loop. Mining these minerals also brings water use, toxic tailings and (for cobalt) sourcing concerns; lithium-ion recycling is improving but is not yet at scale in South Africa. See the report for sources and the even-handed comparison with ICE's own non-CO₂ burdens.

Charging speed and battery life (slow AC vs rapid DC)

Rapid DC charging is increasingly favoured to cut charging time, but forcing high current into the pack drives heat and lithium plating, which age the traction battery faster. The effect is modest for well-cooled packs charged occasionally, but grows with heavy reliance, high state-of-charge fast charges and heat, so South Africa's warm climate and cheaper, less-actively-cooled EVs sit at the more damaging end. Faster ageing brings the battery (or whole-vehicle) replacement forward: an added cost, and an added burden in the mining and disposal value chain that a running-cost or tailpipe view never shows. Evidence, in brief: an Idaho National Laboratory road test of identical Nissan Leafs found about 24.5% capacity loss on slow (Level 2) charging against about 27% on DC fast charging over 50,000 miles; other studies put the extra loss from heavy fast charging at roughly 3 to 9 percentage points, while well-cooled packs (for example Tesla) can show little difference. Heat is the main amplifier, and high charge rates promote lithium plating that permanently cuts capacity. These are modelled estimates for illustration, tunable with the "DC fast-charge share" slider, not a manufacturer warranty figure.

What the model is saying right now

How the 5 / 10 / 15 / 20-year costs are calculated

The model runs year by year and adds up the running total to the end of each horizon. Every horizon is the same six building blocks, only the number of years (and so the compounding) changes:

  1. Capital (depreciation, not sticker price). The purchase price minus what the vehicle is still worth at the end of the horizon. Resale value falls with age on a per-type curve, EVs fastest, ICE slowest, so over 5 years a car gives back most of its price, but over 20 years almost none. This is why a R750k EV adds only ~R333k of capital at 5 years.
  2. Vehicle replacement. If a vehicle reaches its lifespan before the horizon ends, it is retired and a new one is bought (for an EV under the hard cliff the old one is worth R0). That adds a second purchase price and a second slug of manufacturing CO₂, the main reason EV totals jump between 10 and 15 years.
  3. Energy. Fuel or electricity for that year's kilometres, priced at the escalated rate for the year, petrol and electricity compound at the escalation sliders, so later years cost more. The solar EV pays almost nothing here; its energy cost sits in the solar-system line instead.
  4. Insurance + service. Annual amounts, rising each year at general inflation.
  5. Tyres. A fresh set is charged each time cumulative distance crosses the tyre interval (shorter for heavier EVs), at the inflated price of that year.
  6. Home solar system (solar EV only). The upfront cost is booked in year 1, with a storage-battery replacement roughly every 12 years.

The CO₂ table is built the same way, manufacturing carbon for each build (including any replacement), operating carbon (petrol burned, or grid kWh × grid intensity; solar ≈ 0), tyre-manufacturing carbon, and the solar system's footprint. Why longer horizons punish the grid EV: escalation compounds, more tyre sets are consumed, and a write-off forces a second build, so the gap widens with time rather than closing. The "forecast to 2035" chart simply plots this cumulative running total for each calendar year.

Choosing inputs if towing / heavy-duty is a requirement

  • Turn on the “Heavy-duty / towing” toggle. It lifts energy draw ~60%, shortens tyre life ~30%, and caps EV life near 9-10 years, the realistic picture for a vehicle hauling loads, a caravan or a boat.
  • Do not use the budget city-car EV price (~R340k). Those EVs (e.g. BYD Dolphin Surf, ~232 km range, 55 kW) are not rated to tow. For a tow-capable EV set the EV price realistically high (R900k+ for an EV bakkie or large SUV, of which there are very few in SA).
  • Consider switching the hard 10-yr cliff on for utility EVs. Heavy use degrades the traction battery faster; once it drops below ~70% state of health, towing range and payload utility fall away and write-off risk climbs.
  • Stress-test the energy story. Towing can nearly double consumption, so push mileage up and keep electricity escalation high to see how exposed a grid-tied utility EV becomes.
  • Pragmatic read. For genuine towing/heavy-duty work in SA today, a diesel/petrol bakkie or a hybrid is usually the sensible choice; the EV case only holds for the solar-charged option, and only if it survives long enough to amortise its much larger upfront cost.
